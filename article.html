Wayne Andersen, Marcel Duchamp: The Failed Messiah

by Francis M. Naumann Published: 2010/07/10 · Updated: 2026/01/15

[Editorial note: this is placeholder body copy demonstrating the article template's layout and typography. It is original text written for this recreation, not the journal's published review.]

When a book arrives with a subtitle as combative as The Failed Messiah, it announces its argument before the reader has turned a single page. Andersen's central claim — that decades of scholarship have overstated Duchamp's influence on the trajectory of twentieth-century art — is not new, but the intensity with which he pursues it here is.

The strongest sections of the book are the ones least concerned with polemic: close, patient readings of specific works that trace how an idea moved from notebook to object. It's in these passages that Andersen's decades of familiarity with the archive pay off, regardless of where one lands on his larger thesis.

"Placeholder pull-quote demonstrating the blockquote treatment used throughout the journal's article template."

A note on method

Where the book is less convincing is in its treatment of secondary sources, which are often marshaled to support a conclusion already reached rather than tested against it. Readers coming to Duchamp for the first time may find the confidence of the prose persuasive; readers already steeped in the literature will likely spend more time in the footnotes arguing back.

Whatever one makes of its conclusions, the book is a useful reminder that Duchamp scholarship remains an active, contested field — not a settled one.
